### Robert Hand - Horoscope Symbols

#### Hand Factor Entry

- Passage locator: PDF pp. `108–109`; no printed folio visible. The source calls this the Aries Point; the existing wiki rendering uses the canonical name Vernal Point. PDF p. `110` begins a different section.

The first point of Aries, 0° Aries, the vernal point, or the vernal equinoctial point is, as defined in Chapter 2, where the Sun is at the astronomical beginning of spring, about March 21. It, too, is a form of node: the north node of the ecliptic with respect to the equator. Where the Sun crosses the equatorial plane going north is 0° Aries; the south node is 0° Libra. As the beginning of the tropical zodiac, 0° Aries has been used since the ancient Greeks and the dawn of modern horoscopic astrology. Whereas in most astrological systems it has been treated only as the beginning of the sign Aries, however, it was given a new role in the 1920s and 1930s in the Uranian system of Alfred Witte and his followers. According to Uranian usage, 0° Aries represents 0° of all the other

cardinal signs (Cancer, libra, and Capricorn) as well. The reasons for this will become clear in Chapter 8. I have found the Vernal Point extremely useful. Although it is not an energy source like a true planet, the Vernal Point is treated like a planet in that it receives and gives aspects in the normal manner. Most important, it can be the focus of midpoint combinations (see Chapter 8). According to the Uranian school, the Vernal Point, Moon's nodes, and Ascendant represent a hierarchy of relationships. Whereas the Uranians say that the Ascendant-Descendant axis relates to general social interactions, and the lunar nodes to more intimate kinds of meetings, my own experience indicates that the Ascendant-Descendant axis signifies the more intimate contacts. I do agree, however, that the lunar nodes often signify relationships that come about because the individuals feel they have something in common, a feeling that has its roots in lunar symbolism. Of the three nodal points of relationship, the Vernal Point represents the most impersonal but also the widest social contacts: one's relationship to the larger world around one. Hence it is associated with fame and greater social significance. If one ever becomes famous or makes a significant impact on society outside one's circle of friends and associates, it is through symbols that relate to the Vernal Point. Nevertheless, a large number of aspects and/or planetary pictures or midpoint combinations to the Vernal Point does not in itself ensure fame. The Vernal Point and the points connected to it only tell what energies are available to one by which to make an impact should one ever wish to try. Involvements concerning the Midheaven and the tenth house are much more likely than an active Vernal Point to indicate that a person will try to become famous. The reason is that an active Midheaven signifies people who have a strong ideal of significance that they must attain. In this respect, the Midheaven is strongly connected to the father complex of symbols, which includes the Sun, Saturn, Capricorn, the tenth house, and, to some extent, Jupiter. Not only should the Vernal Point itself be checked, but also its midpoints with the personal points: the Midheaven, Ascendant, Sun, and Moon.

## Comparative Schema

- core meaning: the general public, public life, the world, and one's impersonal relationship with the wider social environment ([Witte](#witte-factor-entry), [ABC](#abc-keyword-entry), [Hand](#hand-factor-entry)).
- psychology: these entries describe the field in which a person can make an impact, rather than a standalone personality type. Hand separates available means of public impact from the motivation to seek fame ([Hand](#hand-factor-entry)).
- body/health: no specific bodily or medical correspondence is established by these entries. Witte's `Telluric (terrestrial) influences` refers to an earthly scope without supplying a health claim ([Witte](#witte-factor-entry)).
- social/relationship: public and impersonal contacts beyond the circle of friends and associates; Witte includes people one deals with superficially and the street ([Witte](#witte-factor-entry), [Hand](#hand-factor-entry)).
- events/manifestations: publicity, fame, and wider social significance are possible expressions of public contact; Hand explicitly says that many combinations involving the point do not ensure fame ([Hand](#hand-factor-entry)).
- conflicts/notes: Hand reports a disagreement about the relative intimacy of Ascendant and Node contacts, while retaining the Vernal Point as the most impersonal of the three. This factor is a reference point associated with the cardinal cross, not a planetary energy source ([Witte](#witte-factor-entry), [Hand](#hand-factor-entry)).

## Contradictions and Framework Notes

- Hand's disagreement concerns the relative intimacy of Ascendant and Node contacts; it does not dispute the Vernal Point's public and impersonal scope.
- These entries establish no general psychological liability merely from impersonal contact.

## Derived Synthesis

The Vernal Point describes contact with the wider world: public life, people beyond one's close circle, and the impersonal setting in which an influence can acquire broader social significance. Witte's street and superficial dealings make this scope concrete; his terrestrial influences also retain a connection with the earthly environment. It represents the starting points of the four cardinal signs and is a reference point rather than a planetary energy source. ([Witte, PDF p. 27](#witte-factor-entry), [ABC, p. 16](#abc-keyword-entry), [Hand, PDF pp. 108–109](#hand-factor-entry))

Public reach can include fame or a significant social impact, but that outcome is not assured. Hand distinguishes the energies available for making an impact from a person's motivation to pursue it, and explicitly rejects the idea that many combinations with the Vernal Point ensure fame. The useful distinction is therefore between the public field of contact and any particular ambition or result within it. ([Hand, PDF pp. 108–109](#hand-factor-entry))
